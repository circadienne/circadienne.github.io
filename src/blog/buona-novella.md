Italy has a proud tradition of singer-songwriters, in Italian called _cantautori_ (a pormanteau of _cantatore_ ‘singer’ and _autore_ ‘author’). Many consider the best representative of this tradition to be Fabrizio de André, known by the nickname _Faber_. The word _faber_ (from Latin ‘smith; maker; artisan’) is a reference to de André’s favourite brand of pencils, Faber-Castelli, but is also a reference to his first name (_Fabrizio_ sounds similar to the Italian _fabbro_ ‘smith’) and to his status as a poet (via the metaphor <span style="font-variant: small-caps">Poet as Maker</span>; see Andrew Hurley’s note to “The Maker” in his translations of Borges’s _Collected Fictions_). In fact, while some of his songs are very musically interesting, his melodies are for the most part very simple and what makes them so beautiful are his poetic texts.

After the 1968 student revolutions, while his contemporaries write songs and release albums celebrating or in some way commenting on them, de André begins work on an album retelling Jesus’s life.[^1968] He ultimately releases it in 1969 with the title _La buona novella_ (‘The Good News’), a literal translation of the Greek _<span style="font-family: serif">εὐαγγέλιον</span>_ (_evangelion_, ‘good news; Gospel’).

While he wasn’t an atheist, De André wasn’t exactly a Christian. Many of his songs, such as _“Si chiamava Gesù”_ (‘His Name Was Jesus’), refer to his disbelief in Jesus being the son of God:

> _Non intendo cantare la gloria_
> _Né invocare la grazia e il perdono_
> _Di chi penso non fu altri che un uomo_
> _Come dio passato alla storia…_
>
> I don’t mean to celebrate the glory
> Or invoke the mercy and forgiveness
> Of whom I think was a mere man
> Who went down in history as a god…

His point of view on the New Testament is humanist. While he doesn’t believe in Jesus’s divinity, he sees him as a heroic figure who practiced what he preached—forgiveness and love of all men—to a truly superhuman extent. The song quoted above continues with these lines:

> _…Ma inumano è pur sempre l’amore_
> _Di chi rantola senza rancore_
> _Perdonando con l’ultima voce_
> _Chi lo uccide fra le braccia d’una croce_
>
> …But nevertheless, inhuman is the love
> Of him who groans \[while dying] without resentment
> Forgiving with his last breath
> Those who kill him in the embrace of a cross

This conception of Jesus the Man as well as his deep reverence for him come to the fore in _La buona novella_. The narrative of the Gospels is inserted between the introductory _“Laudate dominum”_ (from Latin ‘Praise the Lord’) and the closing _“Laudate hominem”_ (‘Praise the Man’) and told through the lives of what are ordinarily considered secondary characters.

The story opens with _“L’infanzia di Maria”_ (‘Mary’s Childhood’). In all four canonical gospels, Mary comes to us as a full-formed figure. Compared with the fact that she will give birth to the Redeemer, her past isn’t given a second thought. De André, on the other hand, dedicates considerable time to her tragic childhood as related by the apocryphal Gospel of James. As a child, Mary is taken away from her parents and made to grow up in a temple. Before she gets her first period, she is led out and presented in front of a crowd of male inhabitants. The choir’s initial compassionate address to Mary (‘the wind and the season have gone back to their games / but not with you, child kneeling in the temple’) turns into a jumbled mess of exclamations as the excited crowd scrutinises her appearance, falling over themselves in praising her face, her hair, her ‘flesh of paradise’—the tempo rises and makes you feel what Mary must have felt while being ogled by tens of her potential husbands.

Ultimately, a lottery draw assigns her to Joseph: to ‘fingers that are too dry / to cup a rose; / a heart too old / which is already resting.’ Mary’s youth (she is all of twelve years old when she’s married) is underscored in the following song, _“Il ritorno di Giuseppe”_ (‘Joseph’s Return’), where Joseph brings her a wooden doll, so she can ‘return to those games / you had to abandon / when you were too young.’ After she learns that she will give birth to the Redeemer, there is the song _“Ave Maria”_, an ode dedicated to women who have had a similar fate to hers: who only got to be _“femmine per poco / poi madri per sempre”_ (‘girls for a while / then mothers forever’).

Jesus’s birth is foretold in _“Il sogno di Maria”_ (‘Mary’s Dream’), one of the most beautiful songs on the album. Mary dreams of being back in the temple of her childhood, when an angel comes and lets her fly away with him. The description of their flight reminds one of Shevchenko’s poem “The Dream”:

> _Poi d’improvviso mi sciolse le mani_
> _E le mia braccia divennero ali_
> \[…]
> _Volammo davvero sopra le case_
> _Oltre i cancelli, gli orti, le strade_
> _Poi scivolammo tra valli fiorite_
> _Dove all’ulivo si abbraccia la vite_
>
> Then suddenly he untied my hands
> And my arms became wings
> \[…]
> We flew over the houses
> Beyond the gates, gardens, streets
> Then we floated between flowery hills
> Where the olive tree and grapevine hug each other

When she wakes up from this state, which _“forse era sogno / ma sonno non era”_ (‘might have been a dream / but not sleep’), an echo of the angel’s words still hangs in the air: _“lo chiameranno figlio di Dio”_ (‘they’ll call him the son of God’). Repurposing the verb of the first line quoted above (_“mi sciolse le mani”_), the coda begins by saying that the already exhausted word _“si sciolse in pianto”_ (‘melted into tears’).

_“Via della croce”_ (‘_Via Crucis_’) neatly divides the album in two. Jesus’s torment is described only briefly; he is relegated to a side character as the narrative mostly focuses on the weeping widows and the terrified apostles. In the final verses, a description of the poor fluidly melts into an introduction of Titus and Dumachus, the two thieves crucified next to Jesus. For the narrative of _“La buona novella,”_ they are more important and tragic than Jesus himself. In a way, they are closer to the Messiah presaged in Isaiah 53:3: unlike Christ, mourned by the multitude, they are the ones who are “despised and rejected of men”; they are “\[men] of sorrows, and acquainted with grief.” The only ones to mourn the thieves are their mothers, and their grief is perhaps even more justified than Mary’s:

> _“Lascia a noi piangere un po’ più forte_
> _Chi non risorgerà più dalla morte”_
>
> “Let us grieve a little bit louder
> Those who won’t come back to life”

After the crucifiction, Jesus once more disappears. Instead we listen to Titus listing all ten comandments and trying to apply them to his own life. _“Il testamento di Tito”_ (‘Titus’s Will’) with its compassion for the downtrodden inserts itself neatly into de André’s broader poetics:

> _Il quinto dice: Non devi rubare_
> _E forse io l’ho rispettato_
> _Vuotando in silenzio le tasche già gonfie_
> _Di quelli che avevan rubato:_
> _Ma io, senza legge, rubai in nome mio,_
> _Quegli altri nel nome di Dio_
>
> The fifth \[comandment] says that you shouldn’t steal
> And maybe I did respect it
> By quietly emptying the bulging pockets
> Of those who had stolen:
> But I, the outlaw, stole in my own name,
> The others stole in the name of God

This sympathetic portrayal of the poor and of criminals is common in de André’s work. To take an example, compare the lines from ‘Titus’s Will’ with the final lines of _“La città vecchia”_ (‘Old Town’), a song from a different album:

> _Se tu penserai, se giudicherai_
> _Da buon borghese_
> _Li condannerai a cinquemila anni_
> _Più le spese_
> _Ma se capirai, se li cercherai_
> _Fino in fondo_
> _Se non sono gigli, son pur sempre figli_
> _Vittime di questo mondo_
>
> If you think and judge
> Like a good bourgeois
> You’ll sentence them to five thousand years
> Plus compensation
> But if you understand, if you look for them
> All the way down
> They may not be lilies, but they’re still sons
> \[And] victims of this world

Interestingly, prostitutes, one of the most common groups of ‘victims of this world’ which feature in many of de André’s other songs (think of _“Via del campo”_ ‘The Field Road,’ _“Bocca di rosa”_ ‘Rose Mouth’ or _“La città vecchia”_), are almost entirely absent here. The one exception is the description of the above-mentioned widows, who _“ora soffron la pena / di chi perdonò a Maddalena”_ (‘now suffer the woe / of those who forgave Magdalene’). Except for this topic, however, the album contains all of the most important hallmarks of de André’s _œuvre_. What distinguishes it from the others (in my view) is its pathos and beautiful lyricism.

[^1968]: He would later release an album that dealt with the student revolution: _“Storia di un impiegato”_ (‘The Story of an Employee’).
